How Your Phone Knows Where You Are, With or Without GPS

Without GPS, your phone finds itself from the Wi-Fi networks and cell towers around it, by looking up where they are in a crowd-sourced database.

Short answer

Your apps don't ask the GPS chip where you are. They ask the operating system, the core software that runs the phone (iOS or Android). It combines satellites, nearby Wi-Fi networks, cell towers and Bluetooth, and on Android motion sensors too, then gives each app a position.

GPS needs a view of the sky, so it struggles indoors. Apple says that when buildings block the satellites, your device uses Wi-Fi or cellular networks until it can see them again. In a 2011 Q&A, Apple said an iPhone can work out its location from Wi-Fi and cell tower data alone, such as indoors or in basements. Cell towers give the roughest answer: Google Maps Help says they can be accurate up to a few thousand metres.

One location, several sources

On iPhone, this system is called Location Services. Apple says it uses GPS and Bluetooth where available, plus crowd-sourced Wi-Fi hotspots and cell towers.

On Android, the developer guide points app makers to the fused location provider. It is part of Google Play services, a set of Google services on Android devices (not the Play Store). The guide says it manages the underlying location technology for the app. A phone-wide setting called Location Accuracy lets the phone use more inputs. Google lists GPS, Wi-Fi access points and cell towers, plus the accelerometer, barometer and gyroscope.

Satellites: GPS and other GNSS

GPS (the Global Positioning System) is the United States' satellite system. GNSS (global navigation satellite system) is the general term. GPS.gov lists others, including Europe's Galileo, Russia's GLONASS and China's BeiDou.

GPS.gov describes a one-way system. The satellites transmit their position and the time, and the receiver in your phone uses those signals to calculate its own position. GPS.gov compares the satellites to lighthouses that track nothing on the ground. That settles two myths:

  • GPS itself doesn't send your position anywhere. The GPS receiver only listens to the satellites. What the phone then does with your position is a separate question. Apple, for example, says that while you're in transit with Location Services on, a GPS-enabled iPhone periodically sends Apple GPS locations, speed and direction for its crowd-sourced traffic databases.
  • GPS doesn't need the internet. A network can still help the phone get its first position, called a fix, sooner. In a 2011 Q&A, reproduced by Engadget, Apple said GPS alone can take up to several minutes, and Wi-Fi and cell tower data could cut that to a few seconds.

What GPS does need is a view of the sky. Apple says walls, vehicle roofs, tall buildings and mountains can block it. Two official accuracy figures exist:

  • GPS.gov: GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within a 4.9 m (16 ft) radius under open sky, and worse near buildings, bridges and trees.
  • Google Maps Help: satellites let Maps know your location up to around 20 metres. GPS is sometimes inaccurate inside buildings or underground.
These figures come from different sources and conditions. GPS.gov's is for open sky, and Google's is one round number with no conditions. Don't average them.

Wi-Fi positioning

A Wi-Fi access point is the box that broadcasts a Wi-Fi network, such as a home router. Your phone notes which access points are nearby and looks them up in a database of where they are.

The database is crowd-sourced, meaning ordinary devices build it:

  • Apple says that with Location Services on, your device periodically sends Apple the geo-tagged (position-labelled) locations of nearby Wi-Fi hotspots and cell towers, encrypted and without identifying you.
  • Google says that with Location Accuracy on, it periodically collects where devices observe wireless signals. If many devices see an access point shortly after seeing GPS signals, Google learns it is near there. Google uses a rotating identifier that isn't tied to a person or account.

This is why Wi-Fi works indoors: the phone needs the networks around it, not the sky. Apple says devices without a cellular connection, such as Wi-Fi-only iPads, use only Wi-Fi for Location Services.

To keep your access point out of these databases, rename the network (its SSID) so it ends in _nomap, for example HomeWiFi_nomap. Apple and Google both document this opt-out.

Cell towers

Apple's and Google's databases also cover cell towers. With mobile signal, your phone can estimate where it is from the towers around it. Google Maps Help says this can be accurate up to a few thousand metres. That is the roughest figure here, but towers need no view of the sky.

Apple's 2011 Q&A calls this triangulating. None of the sources here describe the exact calculation, so think of it as an estimate from the known positions of nearby towers.

Bluetooth beacons and motion sensors

Apple says Bluetooth works with iBeacons to create and monitor areas: a beacon marks out a place a phone can recognise. Google lists the accelerometer (movement), gyroscope (rotation) and barometer (air pressure), and says the result can include elevation or floor level.

Why the blue dot is sometimes wrong

  • The circle shows the doubt. Apple Maps and Google Maps both draw a blue circle around the dot when they're unsure. Google says the smaller the circle, the more certain the app is.
  • GPS hasn't settled. Apple says finding all visible satellites can take several minutes.
  • Signals are blocked or bounce. GPS.gov lists buildings, bridges, trees, indoor use and signals reflected off walls, which it calls multipath.
  • A helper is off. Google Maps suggests turning on Wi-Fi and Location Accuracy, and turning off Battery saver if the dot jumps around.
  • A simulated location is still set. See how to get your real location back.

What happens when GPS is off

On current phones, Apple's and Google's help pages don't describe a switch for GPS alone. Apple's page is titled "Turn Location Services and GPS on or off", but its steps have no separate GPS switch. On Android 12 and later, turning off Location Accuracy keeps GPS and drops Wi-Fi and cell towers. Older Android did have a switch for GPS alone. On Android 4.1 to 4.3, you could pick GPS satellites and Wi-Fi and mobile network location separately. Android 4.4 to 8.1 had a location Mode setting. Its Battery saving option used lower-power sources such as Wi-Fi and mobile networks, and its Device only option used GPS and sensors.

If your phone found you when you thought GPS was off, check these explanations:

  1. GPS had no view of the sky, and Wi-Fi or cell towers answered instead.
  2. You turned off something else, such as Location Accuracy, which keeps GPS, or Precise Location, which still leaves an approximate location.
  3. Location was off, and a website used your IP address, as the next section explains.

Location from outside your phone

An IP address is the address your internet connection uses to reach websites. Google says IP addresses are roughly based on geography, so any website you use may learn your general area. This works even with location off: Google says you can still get local results and ads based on it. Apple's Mac guide adds that apps and websites may use other ways to find you. It is a rough area, not a position: Apple describes matching an IP address to a geographic region.

Photos are another route. Apple says that when the Camera app has location access, it embeds coordinates in each photo and video. Our precise vs approximate guide shows how to remove them.

Where a simulated location fits

Developers and testers sometimes need a phone to report another place to test an app. Both platforms support this:

  • iPhone: Apple says you can simulate locations by loading GPX files (a common format for map points and routes) in the Xcode debugger. Xcode is Apple's app-building tool. See our Xcode location guide.
  • Android: Developer options has Select mock location app, which Android says fakes the device's GPS location for testing. See our guide to that setting.

Both replace the answer the operating system gives to apps, and the system can label it. On iPhone (iOS 15 and later), a location's isSimulatedBySoftware value is true when the system generated it using on-device software simulation. On Android, isMock() returns true for a mock location. Android adds that people may have good reasons to mock their location, so apps should generally reject it only when real locations are essential.

So an app that checks can tell. Some apps' terms also forbid changing your location, so read them first.

FAQ

Does GPS use mobile data?

GPS itself doesn't. The satellites send one-way signals, and your phone calculates its position from them. Your phone may use a network to get its first fix faster, and Apple notes that maps and directions depend on data services.

Why does my laptop know where I am without GPS?

Apple says a Mac determines its location using information from local Wi-Fi networks. Microsoft says Windows can use GPS, nearby wireless access points, cell towers, your IP address or a default location. Google notes that most computers can send location information to websites even without GPS.

How accurate is phone GPS?

GPS.gov says GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within a 4.9 m (16 ft) radius under open sky. Google Maps Help says up to around 20 metres.

Can my phone still be located with location turned off?

Apps lose access, but websites can still estimate your general area from your IP address. Apple says your iPhone's location may be used when you place an emergency call. Google says your location can still be sent to emergency responders when you call or text an emergency number. Google also says that with location off, you may not be able to locate your device if you lose it.
