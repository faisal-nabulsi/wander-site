How to Test Geofencing Without Driving Around: iOS and Android, From Simulator to Real Device

To test geofencing without driving, climb a ladder: unit-test the fence logic, replay a route in the iOS Simulator or Android emulator, move a simulated location on a real phone while your app runs in the background, then confirm with one short real walk. Often a geofence that doesn't fire is following a documented platform rule, so check those first.

Published 15 November 2026 · 13 min read

You don't need a car to test a geofence. You need a ladder of tests, from cheap to realistic:

  1. Logic tests with hard-coded coordinates.
  2. The iOS Simulator or Android emulator, replaying a route that crosses the fence.
  3. A real phone with a simulated location, while your app sits in the background.
  4. One real walk to confirm what simulation can't show.

What you're actually testing

A geofence is a virtual circle on the map: a centre point plus a radius. Your app asks the operating system to watch it and report when the phone crosses the edge. Apple calls this region monitoring. Its newer CLMonitor API calls it condition monitoring, "also known as geofencing".

There are three events:

  • Enter: the phone moves from outside the circle to inside.
  • Exit: the phone moves from inside to outside.
  • Dwell: the phone stays inside for a set time. Android has a dwell transition with a "loitering delay" you choose. The iOS pages cited here describe only enter and exit, so on iPhone you time the dwell yourself.

Test each event in four app states: foreground, background, terminated (swiped away) and after a reboot. That is twelve cases before permissions and network problems. Driving doesn't scale.

Why geofences don't fire: the platform rules

Android (Google Play services)

Google's geofencing guide lists these rules:

  • Network location, not GPS. On most devices the geofence service uses only network location (position from Wi-Fi and cell signals).
  • Wi-Fi off can mean no alerts, depending on the radius, device model and Android version.
  • No data connection, maybe no alerts. Without a reliable one, alerts might not be generated, because network location needs it.
  • Alerts are late. Usually under 2 minutes, about 2 to 3 minutes under background location limits, and up to 6 minutes if the device has been still for a long time.
  • Small fences are unreliable. For best results, Google recommends a minimum radius of 100 to 150 metres.
  • Already inside fires at once. With INITIAL_TRIGGER_ENTER (set through setInitialTrigger), the service sends an enter event when the fence is added if the device is already inside it.
  • 100 geofences per app, per device user.
  • Re-register when needed. After a reboot, a reinstall, cleared app data, cleared Google Play services data, or a GEOFENCE_NOT_AVAILABLE alert.
  • Permissions. ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION, plus ACCESS_BACKGROUND_LOCATION when targeting Android 10 or higher.

iOS (Core Location)

  • 20 at a time. No app can monitor more than 20 conditions at once.
  • Approximate location turns it off. With reduced accuracy, your app can't use region monitoring. See precise vs approximate location.
  • Relaunch needs Always. iOS tries to launch a non-running app when a condition is met. But it doesn't launch an app that only has When In Use permission to deliver new updates.
  • Recreate the monitor with the same identifier after a relaunch.
  • Reboots are ambiguous. Apple says the system monitors conditions "until the device reboots", and also that monitoring can only happen after the user unlocks the device following a reboot. Don't rely on either reading: recreate your monitors at every launch, then check them after a reboot and unlock.
  • Already inside doesn't count. Apple's archived guide says that if the device is already inside a region when you register it, iOS sends no event. Call requestState(for:) to check. This is the opposite of Android's initial enter trigger.
  • Cross with a margin, then stay. The same guide says iOS reports a crossing only after the location has moved a minimum distance past the boundary and stayed there for at least 20 seconds. For testing, it says, you can assume that distance is about 200 metres.
  • Network and time. Apple's older startMonitoring(for:) reference says timely reports need network connectivity. In a note written for iOS 6 on iPhone 4S or later, it quotes 3 to 5 minutes on average, if not sooner.

Rung 1: logic tests

Apple's guide to simulating location in tests says you don't need a location manager to test location logic. Build coordinates with known values and pass them straight to your code. Do the same on Android: test your "inside, and for how long?" function with points inside, outside and on the edge.

Rung 2 on iPhone: the iOS Simulator

Use the Simulator to check that callbacks are wired up. Feed it a GPX file (a standard XML format for GPS tracks) that starts outside the fence, crosses it and leaves. Apple's archived testing guide says that, for testing, you can assume iOS waits until the location is about 200 metres past the boundary for at least 20 seconds. So for a 150-metre fence on the middle point, the route starts and ends about 445 metres from the centre (150 + 200 metres, plus some spare). It pauses for a minute at the centre, because a 150-metre fence has no point 200 metres inside it, and it holds the final point for five minutes so the exit has time to arrive:

<?xml version="1.0"?>
<gpx version="1.1" creator="geofence-test">
  <wpt lat="37.3280" lon="-122.0300"><time>2026-01-01T10:00:00Z</time></wpt>
  <wpt lat="37.3320" lon="-122.0300"><time>2026-01-01T10:05:00Z</time></wpt>
  <wpt lat="37.3320" lon="-122.0300"><time>2026-01-01T10:06:00Z</time></wpt>
  <wpt lat="37.3360" lon="-122.0300"><time>2026-01-01T10:11:00Z</time></wpt>
  <wpt lat="37.3360" lon="-122.0300"><time>2026-01-01T10:16:00Z</time></wpt>
</gpx>

TELUS Digital's guide explains the timing: Xcode moves at the speed implied by the gaps between waypoint times, and waypoints must be in time order. Here, about 445 metres every 5 minutes is walking pace.

  • While debugging: turn on Allow Location Simulation in your scheme's Run options, then pick the file from Xcode's Simulate Location menu. Menu names move between Xcode versions.
  • In test plans: set a Simulated Location or GPX file in the test plan's configuration. Xcode replays location, elevation and speed. In UI tests, use XCUIDevice instead.
  • From the command line: xcrun simctl location booted set 37.3320,-122.0300 sets a point and xcrun simctl location booted clear resets it. Since Xcode 14, simctl can also play routes; xcrun simctl help location lists the options.

Two gotchas from Stack Overflow. One developer's didEnterRegion never fired because their code requested When In Use in the Simulator but Always on the phone. And if nothing fires, set a custom location right at the region's centre to prove the wiring. If the Simulator ignores your GPX file, see Xcode simulate location not working.

Rung 2 on Android: the emulator

Open Extended controls (the More button in the emulator panel) and choose Location:

  • Single points: search or click the map, then press Set location.
  • Routes: build and save a route, then press Play route. You can turn on Repeat playback and change the Playback speed.
  • Load GPX/KML: play a track file at 1X speed or faster.
  • From the command line: adb emu geo fix -122.0300 37.3320 sends a GPS fix to the running emulator. Longitude comes first. Because it is one shell command, you can script a route as a series of fixes with pauses between them.

If the map is blank, update the emulator. Versions before 34.2.13 ship an old Chromium that breaks it (see Google's troubleshooting page and issue 228201899). Also, geofences are kept in a Google Play services process, so use a system image (the Android build the emulator runs) that includes Google Play services.

Rung 3: a real phone with a simulated location

Simulators don't behave like a phone in a pocket. Some limits do show up in an emulator: Google says Android's background location limits apply to all apps on devices running Android 8.0 (API 26) or higher, so an emulator on API 26 or later has them too, and background geofence events arrive about every couple of minutes. But a real phone adds real hardware, the maker's own battery settings and a real reboot-and-unlock cycle. The goal here is to move the phone's location while your app sits in the background, with no debugger attached.

Use simulated locations on your own apps and test devices. Some apps' terms forbid changing your location, and following them is your responsibility.

Android

Turn on Developer options and choose an app under Select mock location app. If yours is missing, see Android mock location app not showing.

One catch matters for geofences. The geofence service mostly uses network location, so we'd expect that a mock which only feeds the GPS provider might not reach it. That's our inference from Google's guide, not something Google states. What Google does state is that the fused location provider's mock mode reaches geofencing. (The fused location provider is the Google Play services API that apps call to get the device's position.) Its FusedLocationProviderClient reference says mock mode affects every app that uses it, and other location features, including geofencing.

So the most direct free route is to feed the fused location provider yourself, from a debug build or a small helper app:

  1. Declare android.permission.ACCESS_MOCK_LOCATION in your debug build's manifest.
  2. Install the app and choose it under Select mock location app. Google says both steps are required on Android 6.0 and later.
  3. Call setMockMode(true) on FusedLocationProviderClient.
  4. Send setMockLocation() fixes along your route. Give each one a later timestamp than the last, because Google says the provider may expect increasing timestamps.
  5. Call setMockMode(false) when you finish. Mock mode affects every app on the phone that uses the fused provider, so Google says to always switch it off.

If you use any other tool, check how it injects locations, then confirm it with a test fence on your device. That includes Wander: its Android app uses the built-in mock-location developer setting, and we haven't published a test of it against Play services geofencing.

A 2015 Stack Overflow answer adds that the geofence manager considers location requests from every app on the phone, so real positions from other apps can fight your mock ones. It is old, so treat it as a lead to check rather than current behaviour.

iPhone

The simplest route is Xcode: with the iPhone connected, pick a location or GPX file from Simulate Location while Xcode runs your app. This already changes the location for the whole phone, not just your app. TELUS Digital suggests opening Maps to make the system update, and warns the device may keep the simulated location after you disconnect; a restart clears it. The catch is that you drive it from a debug session on your Mac, and an attached debugger is not how users run your app.

Other tools set the same system location through Apple's developer services, with no debugger attached:

  • pymobiledevice3 (free, open source) sets a point or plays a GPX file on iOS 17 and later, for example pymobiledevice3 developer dvt simulate-location play route.gpx, and ... simulate-location clear resets it. It runs on your computer, so keep the phone connected while a route plays.
  • libimobiledevice's idevicesetlocation (free, open source) sets a single point, but its source code says it isn't supported on iOS 17 and later.
  • Wander sets the location from the phone itself, over Apple's own developer tunnel (the connection Apple's developer tools use to reach a phone). After a one-time setup from a Mac or Windows PC (Developer Mode, a free Apple ID and the free LocalDevVPN tunnel app from the App Store; see the install guide), no computer is needed, and scheduled moves keep going in the background. See Wander on iPhone.

Expect iOS to mark all of these fixes as simulated, Xcode's included. Apple's CLLocationSourceInformation.isSimulatedBySoftware is true for locations simulated on the device, and Wander's own pricing FAQ says iOS marks locations set through the developer tunnel as simulated. If your app checks that flag and rejects simulated fixes, these tests will exercise that rejection path rather than your normal one, so plan a separate test for each.

An honest limit: we haven't verified whether iOS relaunches a terminated app when a simulated location meets a condition. A 2015 Stack Overflow question about testing with the app killed has no answers either. Test it, and let the real walk decide.

Designing test routes

  • Start well outside, at least twice the radius or the radius plus 200 metres from the centre, whichever is larger, so accuracy errors can't put you inside and iOS sees a clear crossing.
  • Cross at walking pace. Teleports are a useful test too, but users don't move that way.
  • Enter, dwell, exit, re-enter. The second entry finds state bugs the first one hides.
  • Test near the minimum radius, 100 to 150 metres on Android.
  • Hold, then wait. Stay at each point for at least 20 seconds (iOS needs that), and allow several minutes per step.
  • Log expected and actual events, with times.

Here is how Wander covers each step, as one example (details from features and pricing):

  • Teleport in, then back out: drop a pin, search by name or paste coordinates. That takes two teleports, and the free plan includes 1 a day, so use one of the 3 monthly two-spot routes for the in-and-out test instead.
  • A two-point route through the fence in walking mode, at realistic speed. The free plan includes 3 two-spot routes a month.
  • A multi-stop route for enter, exit and re-enter, with auto-loop for soak tests (long, repeated runs that shake out slow failures). Multi-spot routes are Pro.
  • An itinerary step such as "stay 1 hour here, then route to there" for dwell tests.
  • Route files. Android imports GPX, KML, GeoJSON and CSV; the iPhone app can import route files. GPX import is Pro.

The pricing page marks teleports, two-spot routes, multi-spot routes and GPX import. It doesn't list auto-loop, itineraries or the other import formats, so check pricing or the app for which of these are Pro.

Wander's Geofence Triggers feature is a different thing. It returns your phone to its real GPS when you actually arrive at or leave a real place, so it isn't for testing your app's fences.

Edge cases to script

  • App swiped away. Run the route with the app terminated.
  • Reboot. Android apps must listen for boot-complete and re-register. On iOS, unlock first, then check your app recreated its monitors, since Apple's wording on what survives a reboot is ambiguous.
  • Permission downgraded to While Using, then approximate. Your app should notice and explain.
  • Background App Refresh off (iOS). Apple's archived guide says the system doesn't wake your app for region notifications when Background App Refresh is off, for the whole device or just for your app. Test both.
  • Low Power Mode and Battery Saver. We found no platform numbers to cite, so record what happens.
  • Airplane mode, Wi-Fi off, no data. Android may give no alerts; iOS needs a network for timely reports.

Rung 4: what simulated tests can't tell you

A simulated fix is an exact coordinate. Real positions are noisy. Google says Wi-Fi location is usually accurate to 20 to 50 metres, and without Wi-Fi, on a rural drive say, the error can reach several hundred metres or more. Simulation can't tell you whether your radius survives that. Plan one real test:

  1. Pick a walkable fence with your production radius.
  2. Start well outside, app in the background.
  3. Walk in, wait for your dwell time, walk out.
  4. Log each crossing and each callback, with times.
  5. Repeat after a reboot and with the app swiped away.

On Android, Wander can record that walk and replay the exact track at the pace you walked it (see Wander on Android), so you can rerun the same path later. The replay goes through the same mock-location setting as above, so first confirm that your geofences fire under Wander's mock.

FAQ

Why is didEnterRegion not called in the Simulator?

Check permission first. Request the same authorization in the Simulator as on the phone, and make sure the app has precise location. Then check that the route goes about 200 metres past the boundary and stays there for at least 20 seconds, as Apple's archived testing guide advises, and wait a few minutes. If the location was already inside the region when you registered it, iOS sends no enter event, so call requestState(for:) to check.

Why isn't my geofence triggered with a mock location on Android?

On most devices the geofence service uses network location, so we would expect that a mock which only feeds the GPS provider might not reach it. That is our inference, not something Google states. Google does say the fused location provider's mock mode affects geofencing, so the most direct test is a debug app that calls setMockMode(true) and setMockLocation(). Test any other mock tool against a known fence first.

Can I test geofencing with the app killed?

Partly. Swipe the app away, move the simulated location across the fence and log whether the app wakes. On iOS this needs Always permission. Confirm the result with one real walk.
