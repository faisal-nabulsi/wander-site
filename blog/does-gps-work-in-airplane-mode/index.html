Does GPS Work in Airplane Mode or Without Data? What Your Phone Can Still Do Offline

Usually, yes. Your phone's GPS receiver only listens to satellites, so it needs no mobile data or Wi-Fi, and normally no SIM card either. It normally keeps working in airplane mode too. What suffers offline is how fast you get a first position, and the map, which stays blank unless you downloaded it beforehand.

Published 15 November 2026 · 9 min read

Short answer: Apple's and Google's airplane-mode help pages never mention GPS, and some older phones cut GPS in airplane mode anyway. So the real check is a quick test on your own phone before you rely on it.

Why GPS works without internet, data, Wi-Fi or a SIM

GPS (the Global Positioning System) is the United States' satellite navigation system. GNSS (global navigation satellite system) is the general term for any system like it, including Europe's Galileo and China's BeiDou. This post says "GPS" for all of them.

GPS.gov, the US government's GPS site, describes a one-way system. The satellites send signals that carry their position and the time. A receiver, like the chip in your phone, takes in those signals and calculates its own position. Nothing travels back up.

So the GPS part of your phone needs no mobile network or Wi-Fi, and by the same logic it shouldn't need a SIM. A network only helps with speed, and with the map you see your position on.

What airplane mode actually turns off

  • iPhone. Apple's page, dated February 11, 2026, says Airplane Mode turns off cellular and Wi-Fi, and switches off every radio except Bluetooth. You can still use Wi-Fi and Bluetooth if the airline allows it.
  • Pixel. Google's Pixel help says Wi-Fi and Bluetooth turn off the first time you use Airplane mode. If you turn them back on, the phone remembers next time.
  • Samsung Galaxy. Samsung says Airplane mode turns off calling, texting, mobile data, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and that you can turn Wi-Fi and Bluetooth back on.

None of these pages mentions GPS. Apple's wording about switching off every radio but Bluetooth could be read to include the GPS receiver, but the page doesn't say. For iPhones, the clearest evidence is a 2015 note from a GPS app maker, covered below.

Android's open-source code is more specific. That code, the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), is the shared base that phone makers build their own versions of Android on. Its default list of radios that airplane mode switches off is cell, Bluetooth, ultra-wideband (a short-range radio for finding nearby devices), Wi-Fi and WiMAX (an older wireless broadband standard). GPS isn't on it. That is only a default that phone makers can change, and the forum reports below describe an older phone that cut GPS anyway.

What gets worse offline

The first fix is slower

A fix is the moment your phone has worked out a position. To get one, the receiver needs data describing where each satellite is. Assisted GPS (A-GPS) is a head start: the phone fetches that data, plus the exact time, over a network connection. u-blox, a maker of satellite-positioning chips, says this shortens the time to a first fix.

Offline, unless the phone still has recent satellite data stored, the receiver must read that data from the satellite signals themselves. u-blox says a cold start, with no current satellite data stored, typically takes around 30 seconds in perfect conditions, and can take much longer, or fail, in harder ones. Apple says locating all visible satellites can take several minutes.

Indoors, there is nothing to fall back on

Apple says walls, vehicle roofs, tall buildings and mountains can block the satellites. The device then uses Wi-Fi or cellular networks until it can see them again. In airplane mode with Wi-Fi off, that backup is gone. Google describes the same limit on Android: with its Location Accuracy setting off, only GPS and the phone's sensors are used.

Accuracy depends on your view of the sky

GPS.gov says GPS-enabled smartphones are typically accurate to within 4.9 metres (16 feet) under open sky, and less accurate near buildings, bridges and trees. For the per-app setting that blurs your position on purpose, see precise vs approximate location.

The map is the real problem

A position without map data is a dot on a blank grid. Apple notes that maps and directions depend on data services. So download the map while you still have a connection.

When GPS really won't work in airplane mode

  • Devices without GPS. Apple says GPS is available on iPhone and on iPad Wi-Fi + Cellular models, and that devices without a cellular connection use only Wi-Fi for Location Services. Google says some devices, such as Wi-Fi-only tablets, have no GPS. In airplane mode with Wi-Fi off, a device like that has nothing to locate itself with.
  • iPhones before iOS 8.3. In 2015, Michael Tsai's blog quoted Dave Rullo of the Gaia GPS app: before iOS 8.3, Airplane Mode also switched off GPS on iPhone and iPad, and after the update Gaia GPS could plot you on the map in Airplane Mode. Two commenters disagreed, recalling earlier use of GPS in airplane mode. Treat both as reports, not Apple's word.
  • Some older Android phones. In a 2012 XDA Forums thread, a Motorola RAZR MAXX owner found the phone lost every satellite seconds after airplane mode went on. Other RAZR owners reported the same. They found GPS worked with airplane mode off, even with mobile data off. These are user reports about phones of that era.
  • Location switched off. Apple says apps can't use your location while Location Services is off (Settings > Privacy & Security > Location Services). On Android, check Settings > Location > Use location.
  • Deep indoors or underground. GPS.gov lists both among the common causes of GPS error.

On a plane

These tips come from an accepted 2022 answer on Aviation Stack Exchange, a community Q&A site, so treat them as experience, not official guidance.

  • GPS reception inside an airliner is poor. Holding the phone right up to the window helps.
  • A phone switched on mid-flight starts from a wrong guess about where it is, while moving fast. The first fix can take quite some time.

Download your map before you board, and follow the crew's instructions about when and how you may use your phone.

Run a quick test before your trip

Behaviour differs by phone model and software version, and the makers don't document it. So test your own phone at home. Most of the time goes on waiting.

  1. Download an offline map of where you are. In Google Maps, tap your profile picture, then Offline maps, then Select your own map. In Apple Maps, tap your picture or initials, then Offline Maps.
  2. Turn on airplane mode. On iPhone, open Control Center and tap Airplane Mode. On a Pixel, swipe down from the top of the screen and tap Airplane mode.
  3. Turn Wi-Fi off too. Otherwise the phone may use Wi-Fi to place you, and you learn nothing about GPS. On iPhone, do it in Settings > Wi-Fi. Apple says the Control Center button only disconnects you, and Wi-Fi stays available for Location Services.
  4. Go outside, somewhere with a clear view of the sky.
  5. Open your map and wait. In Google Maps, tap Your location at the bottom right. Give it a few minutes.
  6. Walk 100 metres or so. Check that the dot follows you. A dot that stays put may be left over from before you went offline.
  7. Check the circle around the dot. Google and Apple both say a smaller circle means a more certain position.

If the dot follows you, your phone's GPS works offline. If it is grey or missing, Google says Maps can't find your current location and is showing the last place you were. If nothing changes after several minutes outdoors, your phone may be one of the exceptions above. And remember this is a best case: after a long time offline, the first fix can take longer.

GPS working doesn't mean you're reachable

GPS only brings a signal into your phone. It tells you where you are, and by itself it tells nobody else. With no connection, nobody can call or message you, and live location sharing stops; Google says its Location Sharing needs an active internet connection. Before you go somewhere without coverage, tell someone your route and when you expect to be back. Some phones can reach emergency services by satellite, but that depends on the device and the region.

FAQ

Does GPS use mobile data?

No. GPS satellites send one-way signals, and your phone works out its own position from them. Data is used by the things around GPS: loading maps, traffic and search, and the assistance data that speeds up a first fix.

Does GPS work without a SIM card?

It should, because the GPS receiver only listens to satellites. Without a SIM there is no mobile network to help, so expect a slower first fix and download your maps over Wi-Fi first. Apple points the same way for iPad: it tells owners of Wi-Fi + Cellular models to keep Cellular Data on even without a data plan, so the iPad can calibrate itself using network time and location information. The Apple and Google pages we checked don't cover the no-SIM case directly, so test your own phone.

Does airplane mode turn off location?

Not by itself. It stops location from cell towers, and from Wi-Fi unless you turn Wi-Fi back on. GPS normally keeps working, so apps with permission can still read where you are, though with Wi-Fi and mobile data both off, nothing goes out over the internet until you reconnect. It is not a privacy switch: on iPhone, Bluetooth stays on unless you turn it off, and Apple says its Find My network uses Bluetooth to help locate a device even when it is offline. To stop apps reading your location, turn off Location Services on iPhone or Use location on Android.

Why is my blue dot stuck or grey in airplane mode?

In Google Maps, a grey or missing dot means Maps can't find your current location and is showing the last place you were. In airplane mode, GPS probably has no fix yet and there is no network to fall back on. Go outside, keep the map open and wait a few minutes. If it still won't update, Google's troubleshooting list suggests turning airplane mode off.
